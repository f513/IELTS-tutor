## READING PASSAGE — Ocean Acidification: The Other Carbon Problem

**A** When carbon dioxide dissolves into seawater, it does not simply vanish. Instead, it reacts with water molecules to form carbonic acid, a weak acid that releases hydrogen ions as it breaks apart. These extra hydrogen ions are what make seawater more acidic, pulling down its pH. Since the Industrial Revolution, the ocean has absorbed roughly a third of all carbon dioxide released by human activity, acting as a vast buffer for the atmosphere. This chemical reaction is straightforward enough to demonstrate in a school laboratory, yet its consequences for marine life unfold on a scale that laboratory experiments can only hint at.

**B** Long-term monitoring stations, such as the one established off the coast of Hawaii in the late 1980s, have produced an unusually clean record of this change. Instruments anchored at fixed depths have logged pH readings every few hours for more than three decades, producing a dataset detailed enough to separate seasonal fluctuation from the underlying downward trend. The record shows that the ocean's average surface pH has fallen by about 0.1 units since pre-industrial times. That figure sounds small, but because the pH scale is logarithmic, it represents a roughly 30 percent increase in acidity — a change that would have been difficult to detect without decades of patient, continuous measurement.

**C** Many marine species build hard structures — shells, skeletons, reefs — out of calcium carbonate drawn from seawater. As water becomes more acidic, the carbonate ions these organisms rely on become scarcer, and existing shell material can even begin to dissolve. Juvenile oysters, for instance, must now expend significantly more energy to form a shell of the same thickness their ancestors built decades ago. Coral polyps face a similar burden: a weakened, slower-growing skeleton leaves the reef as a whole more vulnerable to storm damage and more easily overgrown by competing algae. Not every calcifying species is affected equally, but the pattern across most of them is strikingly consistent.

**D** The consequences extend well beyond the organisms themselves. Shellfish aquaculture is a multi-billion-dollar industry in some coastal regions, and several oyster hatcheries on the Pacific coast of North America have already reported mass die-offs of larvae linked to periods of unusually acidic water. Wild fisheries that depend on shell-building prey species lower in the food chain face a less visible but equally serious risk, since a decline at that level can eventually propagate upward to the fish stocks that coastal communities depend on for both income and protein. For some island and coastal nations, this is not a distant, abstract concern but a direct threat to food security.

**E** Acidification is not spreading evenly across the globe. Cold water absorbs carbon dioxide more readily than warm water, which means the polar oceans are acidifying noticeably faster than tropical waters. Researchers monitoring the Southern Ocean around Antarctica have recorded conditions that are already approaching levels once projected only for the second half of this century. This matters because many polar species — tiny swimming snails known as pteropods among them — sit near the base of food webs that support fish, seabirds, and ultimately whales. A disruption that starts small in a remote, cold ocean can therefore still be felt much further up the food chain.

**F** There is no quick chemical fix for a change happening at the scale of an entire ocean, but several approaches are being pursued in parallel. The most direct is simply reducing carbon dioxide emissions at the source, since acidification and climate change share the same root cause. On a more local scale, some coastal authorities have begun restricting agricultural runoff and other pollutants that independently lower coastal water pH, giving vulnerable reefs and shellfish beds some additional breathing room. Separately, breeding programs are selecting shellfish strains that tolerate lower pH more easily. None of these measures reverses acidification on its own, but together they may buy threatened ecosystems valuable time.
