That's a really common plateau, and the good news is it's almost always explainable — "doing lots of tests" and "improving" are not the same activity unless you change *how* you practice. Here's what's most likely going on, roughly in order of likelihood.

## 1. You're probably just taking tests, not analyzing them

Doing 15 full tests tells you *that* you're stuck at 6.5, but it doesn't by itself tell you *why* — and if you're not digging into your wrong answers, you can repeat the same mistake 15 times and never notice the pattern. Band 6.5 (roughly 23–26 correct out of 40) usually means you're losing 14-17 marks, and those losses are rarely random. For every test from now on, don't just mark it and move on — build an error log:

- Question number, section, question type (MCQ, note completion, map, matching, etc.)
- What you wrote vs. the correct answer
- *Why* you got it wrong: didn't hear it / heard it but wrote wrong form / distracted and lost your place / misspelled it / wrong word count / fell for a distractor

After 3-4 tests of doing this, a pattern will almost always jump out — e.g. "I lose almost all my marks in Section 3" or "I keep losing marks to spelling" or "I always lose the question right after a number."

## 2. The specific things that cap people at exactly 6.5

Band 6.5 is a very characteristic ceiling in Listening. The usual suspects, see which ring true:

**a) Spelling and word form errors.** You hear the right answer and write it, but it's marked wrong because of a spelling mistake, wrong plural/singular, or wrong word form (e.g. writing "educate" when the answer needed "education"). This alone can cost 3-5 band-equivalent marks and feels like "I knew it but lost it." Check your answer sheet from recent tests specifically for this — if even half your wrong answers were things you actually heard correctly, this is your main issue, and it's a transcription/proofreading problem, not a listening problem.

**b) Not keeping the word-limit rule automatic.** "No more than two words" / "one word only" — if you're not instantly discarding options that violate this, you're losing free marks. This should be mechanical, not something you think about mid-test.

**c) Distractors in Section 3-4 (and MCQ generally).** IELTS deliberately has speakers say something, then correct themselves or get contradicted by the other speaker ("Actually, no, let's do Thursday instead"). If you're writing down the *first* thing you hear rather than the *final, confirmed* answer, you'll consistently lose marks here. This is probably the single most common reason strong-but-plateaued candidates stay at 6.5 instead of hitting 7.5+.

**d) Losing your place after one miss.** One missed answer causes you to miss the next two because you're still processing the first instead of moving on. If your wrong answers cluster in runs of 2-3 consecutive numbers, this is it. The fix is purely procedural: the instant you're not sure, guess something and move your eyes/attention to the next question — never let one loss cost you three.

**e) Map/plan/diagram labeling (Section 2 especially).** These rely on spatial language (opposite, adjacent to, next to, beyond, on the left as you face it) rather than vocabulary. If you consistently lose 2-3 marks here, it's a specific sub-skill you can isolate and drill separately from general listening.

**f) Weak prediction before the recording starts.** In the real test and in good practice, you get time to read the questions before audio plays. If you're not using that time to predict word type (name? number? place? one word or two?) and underline keywords, you're listening "blind" and relying on recognizing words after the fact — much harder than anticipating them.

**g) Section 4 (the monologue/lecture).** This is the hardest section for almost everyone because there's no second speaker to repeat or clarify information, vocabulary is more academic, and there are no natural pauses between questions apart from the structure of the talk. If your Section 4 score is noticeably worse than 1-3, that's expected and normal — but it's also where extra, targeted practice pays off most.

## 3. A diagnostic you can run today

Pull your last 3-5 completed tests and tally wrong answers by:
- Section (1/2/3/4)
- Question type (form-fill, MCQ, matching, map, note/sentence completion)
- Error type (didn't hear it / misheard / distractor trap / spelling or form / word limit / lost place after previous miss)

Whichever bucket has the most tally marks is your actual problem — and it's very likely *not* "I can't understand spoken English," which is what a stuck score feels like but usually isn't.

## 4. What to change going forward

1. **Stop doing only full tests.** Full tests measure your score; they don't *build* skill efficiently. Mix in section-by-section drilling targeted at your weak bucket from the diagnostic above (e.g., only Section 3s, or only map questions, from several different tests).
2. **Review with the transcript, every time.** For everything you got wrong (and even things you got right but weren't sure of), read the transcript and find the exact sentence containing the answer. Notice the paraphrase — IELTS rarely uses the exact word from the question; it uses a synonym or related phrase. Training your ear to catch paraphrase is probably more valuable than any other single change.
3. **Do a second pass focused purely on spelling/word-limit/form**, separate from listening comprehension — i.e., check your answer sheet against the transcript with the audio off, purely proofreading.
4. **Shadow/dictation practice.** Take a short clip (30-60 seconds) from a test you've already done, play it, and write down literally everything said, word for word, replaying as needed. This builds the fine-grained listening accuracy that skims past you in normal test-speed practice.
5. **Get your timing/attention habits automatic**: predict before audio starts, never dwell on a missed question, always write the *final* confirmed answer not the first-mentioned one, apply the word-limit rule without thinking.
