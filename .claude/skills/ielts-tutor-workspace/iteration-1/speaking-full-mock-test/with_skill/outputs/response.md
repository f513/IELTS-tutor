# IELTS Speaking — Full Mock Test

**Examiner:** Great, two weeks out is exactly the right time to run a full
timed mock rather than drilling isolated questions — let's do Part 1, Part
2, and Part 3 back to back, the way it'll actually run on the day, and I'll
give you proper feedback at the end.

One thing to flag honestly before we start: this is text-based. I can
fully assess your content, how well you develop your answers, your
vocabulary, and your grammar as it shows up in writing — and I can comment
on fluency-of-expression patterns like hesitation, repetition, and
sentence-level flow. What I *can't* do is hear your real pronunciation,
intonation, stress, or accent, so that part of the feedback will be
conceptual rather than a true score. Everything else is a fair proxy.

Let's begin.

## Part 1 — Introduction and Interview

**Examiner:** Do you work, or do you study?

**Student:** I'm currently a student. I study business administration at
university, I'm in my third year, and I'm planning to specialize in
marketing next year.

**Examiner:** Can you tell me a bit more about that?

**Student:** Marketing is more interesting for me than the other business
subjects, because it mixes creativity with strategy. Last semester I did a
project about social media advertising for a small local brand, and I
really enjoyed it, so that's part of why I want to specialize in it.

**Examiner:** Let's talk about your hometown. What's it like?

**Student:** It's a medium-sized city. Pretty normal, nothing special.

**Examiner:** What do you like to do in your free time?

**Student:** In my free time I like playing badminton with my friends —
it's more healthier than just sitting at home, so we usually play twice a
week at a sports center near my house. I also like watching matches on
TV when I don't have time to play myself.

**Examiner:** Do you enjoy cooking?

**Student:** Yes, I enjoy cooking, especially traditional dishes from my
country. I find it relaxing because you can see the result straight away,
and I often cook for my family on weekends, which they appreciate.

**Examiner:** How often do you use your phone?

**Student:** I use it a lot. Mostly for messaging and social media.

## Part 2 — Individual Long Turn

**Examiner:** Now I'm going to give you a topic, and I'd like you to talk
about it for one to two minutes. Before you talk, you'll have one minute
to think about what you're going to say, and you can make notes if you
wish.

**Cue card:**

> **Describe a time when you helped someone who was in need of help.**
>
> You should say:
> - who this person was
> - what the situation was
> - what you did to help them
> - and explain how you felt afterward

**Examiner:** Here's your topic. Remember, you have one minute to prepare.
Go ahead and jot down whatever notes will help you, including anything
beyond just the four bullet points — extra material you might want to
fall back on if you start running low mid-answer.

**Student (prep notes, typed):**
- a classmate who was struggling with a presentation right before exam period
- I helped her organize her slides and practice saying it out loud
- could mention the teamwork skills this connects to, since I want to go into marketing
- how I felt proud afterward, and what it taught me about patience

**Examiner:** Good — that last point about teamwork and the patience angle
are exactly the kind of extra material that keeps you going if the main
story runs short. Whenever you're ready, go ahead and speak for up to two
minutes.

**Student:**
I want to talk about a classmate of mine called Mei, who I helped right
before our exam period last year. She had a big presentation due for one
of our marketing modules, and about two days before it, she told me she
was completely stuck — her slides were messy and she said she felt more
nervous than she'd ever been about presenting in front of the class.

So I offered to help her. First we sat down together and reorganized her
slides so the structure made more sense, because honestly the order was
more confusing than it needed to be. Then we practiced her actually saying
it out loud three or four times, and I gave her feedback on which parts
sounded more unclear than others, so she knew exactly what to fix.

In the end, she did really well in the presentation, and she told me
afterward that she felt a lot more confident than she expected to. I felt
genuinely proud, because I could see the direct difference it made. It
also taught me something about patience — explaining the same thing
several times without getting frustrated — which I think is actually a
useful skill for the kind of marketing and team-based work I want to do in
the future.

**Examiner:** Thank you.

## Part 3 — Two-Way Discussion

**Examiner:** We've been talking about a time you helped someone. I'd like
to discuss some more general questions related to this.

First, why do you think some people enjoy helping others more than other
people do?

**Student:** I think it depends on personality and how someone was
raised. Some people grow up in families where helping others is a strong
value, so for them it becomes natural, whereas other people might focus
more on their own goals — not because they're bad people, just because
they weren't really taught to think about others as much growing up. In
my own case, my parents always volunteered at community events, so
helping people just feels normal to me, it's not something I have to
force myself to do.

**Examiner:** Do you think schools should teach children how to help their
community?

**Student:** Yes, I think it's important, because children are more
easier to influence than adults, so if schools teach this early, it can
shape their mindset for the future, and they'll just carry that habit with
them without really thinking about it.

**Examiner:** Is it more important to help family and friends, or to help
strangers?

**Student:** That's a hard question. I think helping family and friends
is more easier, because you already know them and understand what they
need, but helping strangers is also valuable — maybe even more harder in
some ways, because you don't know their situation, so you have to be more
careful about how you approach it. I don't have a really strong opinion on
which matters more, I'd just say both are important for different reasons.

**Examiner:** Do you think modern society is becoming more caring, or less
caring, compared to the past?

**Student:** Honestly, I'm not very sure about this one, because I haven't
studied it or anything, but I would guess society is more busier now in
general, so people have less time to help each other compared to before.
On the other hand, social media also makes it more easier to organize
charity events and reach a lot of people quickly, so maybe it's not
completely negative, just different.

**Examiner:** Some people say technology makes people less willing to help
each other face-to-face. Do you agree?

**Student:** Yes, I agree a little bit. People message each other instead
of visiting in person, so some face-to-face helping, like checking on an
elderly neighbor, probably happens less now than before. But technology
also makes it more easier to find people who actually need help, like
through online fundraising, so I'd say it's a mix of positive and negative
effects rather than all one way.

**Examiner:** Thank you. That's the end of the speaking test.

## Feedback

Really solid effort to run the whole thing in one go two weeks out — this
is exactly the kind of practice that moves a score, so let's go through it
properly.

### Part-by-part

**Part 1** — Your job/study answer and the cooking answer were genuinely
good: direct answer, one reason, one personal detail — that's the formula
working. But the hometown answer ("It's a medium-sized city. Pretty
normal, nothing special.") and the phone answer ("I use it a lot. Mostly
for messaging and social media.") were both too short. Part 1 isn't a
knowledge test and doesn't need to be deep, but a bare two-line answer
with no example reads as underdeveloped to an examiner no matter how
accurate it is — it's the single most common issue at every band, and it's
the fastest one to fix. One more sentence each — "it's pretty normal, but
I like it because..." — would have fixed both instantly.

**Part 2** — This was your strongest section. You used a real story from
your own life rather than inventing one, which is exactly what this
channel's methodology rewards, and your prep notes did real work: the
teamwork/patience angle you jotted down as "extra ammunition" is what let
you close the answer with a reflective point instead of just stopping once
the story ended. Good use of the full prep step.

**Part 3** — Mixed. Your answer to "why do some people help more than
others" was excellent — proper multi-perspective expansion ("some people
grow up... whereas other people..."), backed by a real personal detail
about your parents. Your answer about society becoming more/less caring
was also handled the right way: you admitted upfront you weren't sure,
but you still gave a full, reasoned answer rather than saying "I don't
know" — that's precisely the right instinct on an unfamiliar question;
remember, it's an English test, not a knowledge test. The weaker moment
was introducing "mindset" in the schools answer without ever unpacking
what you meant by it — on an abstract term like that, define it in the
same answer, because the examiner won't follow up to ask.

### Pronunciation
*(Caveat: inferred from written text only, not heard — treat this as
conceptual, not a real score.)* Nothing in the text suggests comprehension
problems, and your sentence-level flow reads naturally rather than
stiffly. Provisionally **Band 7**, but this needs to be confirmed by
actually listening to you, ideally with a teacher or even just recording
yourself.

### Lexical Resource
Good and accurate throughout — "footfall"-style topic vocabulary wasn't
needed here, but you used genuinely relevant language naturally: "social
media advertising," "community events," "charity events," "online
fundraising." Nothing failed or sounded forced, which tells me you're
staying within the vocabulary you're 100% sure of — that's the right
instinct for test day. The one thing holding you back from a higher score
is range: in Part 3 especially, a lot of your sentences leaned on
comparatives to do the work that a wider range of structures or
expressions could have done. **Band 7.**

### Grammatical Range and Accuracy
This is the main thing to fix in the next two weeks, and it's very
specific. You said "more easier" in Part 3 three times, plus "more
healthier" in Part 1, "more busier," and "more confusing" vs. correctly
unmarked forms elsewhere — the double comparative ("more" + an "-er"
word) wasn't an occasional slip, it showed up nearly every single time
you made a comparison, across four different questions. That's exactly
the systematic-error pattern: an occasional double comparative barely
costs anything, even near Band 9 — everyone has little slips — but a
structure that's wrong almost every time it's used signals you don't yet
control that rule, and it caps this criterion at around **Band 6**,
regardless of how good everything else sounds. The encouraging part: this
is one specific, narrow fix — not a sign your grammar overall is weak.
Elsewhere you handled past narrative tense well in Part 2 and a real
conditional ("if schools teach this early, it can shape...") in Part 3.
**Band 6**, driven entirely by this one recurring pattern.

### Fluency and Coherence
Mostly good — no sign of searching for *language* anywhere, which is the
real fluency marker; your pausing-to-think moments (like admitting
uncertainty before the society question) are about ideas, not language,
and that's not a fluency problem. Coherence is where the two short Part 1
answers and the undefined "mindset" term cost you — development is the
one sub-skill you can improve fastest, and it's also the main thing
separating this performance from a band higher. **Band 7**, but closer to
6.5 once you weigh in the underdeveloped moments.

### Estimated overall band: **Band 6.5**

Scores aren't siloed — you can't float at Band 7 everywhere while one
specific habit (the comparative pattern) keeps resetting Grammar to 6, so
the overall sits closer to the weaker end. The genuinely good news: this
isn't a competence gap across the board. Your ideas, your real-life
material in Part 2, and your Part 3 reasoning were often Band 7-level.
One narrow habit and two short Part 1 answers are doing almost all of the
damage.

### Two concrete next steps for the next two weeks
1. **Kill the double comparative specifically.** Every time you want to
   compare two things, pick one form only — either "more + adjective"
   (more difficult) or "adjective + -er" (harder), never both. Say your
   Part 3 answers out loud again and self-correct every "more easier,"
   "more harder," "more busier" you hear — this single fix is worth more
   to your Grammar score than any new vocabulary right now.
2. **Never let a Part 1 answer stay at one sentence.** Direct answer, then
   exactly one more sentence of reason or example, every time — the
   hometown and phone answers are the template to fix: "it's pretty
   normal, but I like it because..." takes two seconds and changes how
   developed the whole answer sounds.

You're in good shape for a Band 6.5–7 range two weeks out — tighten the
comparative pattern and stretch out the short Part 1 answers, and this
moves up, not because you need new skills, but because you already have
them and just need to apply them consistently.
