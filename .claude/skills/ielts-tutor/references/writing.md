# Writing — reference

Single source of truth for coaching IELTS Writing: Task 1 (Academic report-writing and General Training letter-writing) and Task 2 (opinion/discussion/problem-solution essays). Grounded in the actual methodology of the YouTube channel **IELTS Advantage** (@Ieltsadvantage), not generic IELTS-prep advice.
Provenance: built from the repo's Writing research pass over all 108 Writing videos; tip verdicts come from the only two fully transcribed videos, *Every IELTS Writing Tip Explained in 39 Minutes* (684xymRpBc0) and *IELTS Writing Tips You MUST Know Before Your Test* (p-r65jaSz4o).

## Philosophy

**Simplicity beats sophistication. Lead with this idea more than any other.** It's the strongest, highest-confidence finding in the whole research base. Two separate research passes reached it *independently*: one studied the channel's training/technique videos and the other studied its advice/testimonial videos. Both found it to be their single most-repeated theme. Because evidence from two disjoint sets converged on it, treat it as near-certain rather than as one recurring talking point among many.

Concretely:
- Complex, "fancy," high-level vocabulary **lowers** scores when it's used even slightly inaccurately. It doesn't raise them.
- Heavy use of idioms, phrasal verbs and linking words doesn't impress examiners. It works against students.
- The channel actively discourages brainstorming long lists of ideas. Instead, students should pick one or two ideas they can develop and express with total accuracy.

**Backing data point (transcript-confirmed):** the channel ran **100 real Band 7–9 VIP student essays** through word-frequency analysis. The **top 20 most-repeated words made up 33.5% (about a third) of all the words in those essays.** Use this figure precisely. The channel used it to prove that *repeating common words is normal and isn't a Lexical Resource weakness*. It isn't a blanket "only use simple words" slogan. The channel's real nuance (see Core techniques) is that simplicity is about *accuracy and control*. It doesn't mean banning all advanced vocabulary or limiting students to childish language.

Carry this philosophy into every mode. When a student reaches for something "impressive," your default is to ask two questions: are they 100% certain it's correct, and would a simpler version serve them just as well or better?

## Core techniques

### Task 2 structure — confirmed default skeleton
The channel's default Task 2 skeleton is a **4-paragraph essay**: Introduction → Main Body Paragraph 1 → Main Body Paragraph 2 → Conclusion.
- **Introduction:** paraphrase the question, then briefly state your ideas or position in 3 simple layers (question type → keywords/instruction words → your position). Never open with a memorized "hook," a thesis statement, or an outline sentence that tells the examiner what you're about to say. This is ranked the worst tip in the entire dataset (see Tip-by-tip verdicts).
- **Main body paragraph** = **topic sentence (main idea) → explanation (explain the idea so anyone could understand it) → one relevant example**. The channel explicitly warns that *listing* several ideas without developing any of them lowers Task Response. One well-developed idea beats three underdeveloped ones.
- **Conclusion:** summarize without reusing the introduction's wording (paraphrase, don't copy). Critically, **the conclusion's stance must match the introduction's stance.** Treat intro/conclusion consistency as something concrete you can check when you grade.

**General 5-step plan** sitting above this structure: understand the question → generate ideas → plan the structure → write paragraphs with examples → conclude.

**Time management:** Task 2 takes about 40 minutes. The transcript-confirmed recommended split is roughly **10 minutes planning / 25 minutes writing / 5 minutes proofreading**. Tell students that strict proofreading-time discipline helps most people but isn't universal. The channel ranks it A tier, not S tier, because some students manage time poorly and rushing to "leave time to check" can backfire. Adjust the split if a student's own practice shows otherwise.

**Word count:** **250 words is a hard floor for Task 2.** It's tied directly to the Task Response criterion, and falling short costs marks. Past that point, more isn't better: drifting to 350–450 words correlates with going off-topic and running out of time to proofread or start Task 1. The correct framing is to hit the floor confidently, then stop adding length for its own sake.

### The Coffee Shop Method (idea generation) — confirmed, A tier
This is Chris's own named technique and the channel's confirmed replacement for brainstorming. Under exam stress, the student doesn't try to generate and sort a list of ideas. Instead, they imagine explaining the essay topic out loud to a friend over coffee. They picture a real, relaxed conversation ("I imagine my best friend, and we were in Starbucks, and she asked me...") rather than treating the moment like a test. This removes the "test mode" stress that pushes students toward overcomplicated, academic-sounding ideas, and it brings out natural, usable ideas quickly.

Teach it as the direct alternative whenever a student says they're stuck generating ideas or that they over-brainstorm. Be honest about its limit: the channel ranks it **A tier, not S tier**. Some students find imagining a conversation easy and others find it awkward, so offer it as a tool to try, not a guaranteed fix.

### The 100% Rule (vocabulary and word choice) — confirmed
This is a named VIP-course rule: **only use a word, collocation, or spelling you are 100% sure is correct**, however impressive a riskier alternative sounds. It turns "simplicity beats sophistication" into a concrete rule. It doesn't mean "use only basic words." It means "never gamble on a word you aren't certain of." Use exactly this framing when a student asks whether to use a more advanced word they're unsure of.

### The 3-choice word-repetition rule — confirmed
When a student is tempted to avoid repeating a word, the channel teaches exactly three options:
1. Repeat the word.
2. Change it to a synonym you're **not** sure about.
3. Change it to a synonym you're **100% sure** about.

The popular "never repeat a word" tip pushes students toward option 2 by default, which is the wrong choice. Teach students to pick **only option 1 or option 3, never option 2**. The full reasoning is in Tip-by-tip verdicts.

### KISS — "Keep It Simple, Stupid" / "clarity is king" — confirmed, S tier
Chris calls this his own personal motto ("clarity is king... I came up with that"), and the transcript ranks it **S tier**. Get this distinction right every time you invoke it: **KISS does NOT mean "use simple words."** It means **don't overcomplicate your ideas, essay structure, or grammar.**

The channel reports that very intelligent professionals, such as doctors and engineers, often plateau at Band 6–6.5. Their English isn't the problem. They bring workplace-level conceptual complexity into their essays. The channel's fix is to ask the student: "What's the simplest idea here? Could you explain it to a 10-year-old?" This reportedly fixes those students' writing within days.

Use this reframing whenever a student is overcomplicating an idea or sentence, especially a technically sophisticated student. Keep it separate from vocabulary-level advice, which belongs to the 100% Rule.

## Named systems that are UNCONFIRMED — names only

The channel names the systems below in video titles, but the source research doesn't confirm them. They were checked against the two available full transcripts and weren't found in either. **Don't explain their mechanics** (see Honesty rule).
- **"Family Fortunes Method"**: a Task 2 idea-generation strategy borrowed from the TV game-show concept. Only the name is known; no mechanism was recovered.
- **Numbered step-systems**:
  - the "3-step method" (Plan/Write/Check)
  - the "4-step strategy"
  - the "5-step plan"
  - "5 Steps to a Band 7". This is the *only* numbered system with any real detail, and it's only partly spelled out: understand the question → plan → write intro → write body paragraphs → write conclusion.
  - testimonial labels such as Crystal's "3-step method," Ding's "3 simple rules," and Sana's "4 tips."

  These may all be versions of the same plan→write→check progression, marketed under different names over the years. That's speculation, not a confirmed finding.
- The "3 essential elements" of a main body paragraph and the "3-part system" for a conclusion. The counts are named, but the parts aren't itemized beyond what's given in Core techniques.
- The "3 simple layers" for analyzing a Task 2 question. The sources name them only as question type → keywords/instruction words → position, with no further detail.
- The "one rule" for diagnosing whether a GT letter should be formal or informal.

## Task 1 coverage

### Academic (reports)
The channel teaches one canonical, highly consistent structure:
1. **Introduction:** paraphrase the question.
2. **Overview paragraph:** the 2–3 biggest or most general trends or features, with no numbers.
3. **Details paragraph(s):** specific data, comparisons and numbers.

This applies across chart types: line graphs, pie charts, bar charts, tables, maps and process diagrams. Process diagrams also need process-specific vocabulary and sequencing language instead of trend language.

The channel teaches the scoring criteria first, in this order: Task Achievement → Coherence & Cohesion → Vocabulary → Grammar → then structure. The same order is worth reusing when you grade.

### General Training (letters)
The default skeleton is a **4-paragraph letter structure**, used for both formal and informal letters.

The channel teaches one critical first step: decide whether the prompt calls for a formal or informal letter *before* drafting. The exact content of that step is unconfirmed (see Honesty rule). Get the student to make and justify this call explicitly rather than skip it.

Register-appropriate vocabulary differs sharply between the two:
- **Informal:** contractions are fine, and idioms are actually appropriate here, in line with the channel's idiom guidance.
- **Formal:** no contractions, no idioms, professional phrasing.

### Cross-task reminders
- **Task 1 causes more overall Writing failures than Task 2, even though it's worth only half the marks.** This is a repeated channel claim confirmed across both research batches. Don't let a student (or yourself) under-invest in Task 1 relative to Task 2. If a student's self-study time leans toward Task 2, flag it explicitly.
- Time limits and word minimums: Task 1 is 20 minutes and 150 words; Task 2 is 40 minutes and 250 words.
- The channel also suggests doing Task 2 first (about 40 minutes) and Task 1 second (about 20 minutes). The source presents this as one channel opinion, not settled guidance (see Known gaps).

## Scoring-criteria framing

Teach explicitly to the four official criteria. Each is weighted equally, at 25%.

- **Task Achievement** (the Task 1 term) / **Task Response** (the Task 2 term)
  - Did the response fully address every part of the prompt, with relevant, extended and supported ideas?
  - For Task 1: is the overview accurate, and are the key trends and data covered?
  - The 250-word Task 2 floor is a Task Achievement/Response rule, not a cosmetic one.
- **Coherence and Cohesion**
  - Logical paragraphing and progression of ideas.
  - Linking devices should be used *purposefully, not maximally*.
  - When you explain a verdict, quote the real descriptor language: Band 9 cohesion "attracts no attention"; Band 6 cohesion "may be faulty or mechanical."
- **Lexical Resource**
  - Range, but above all **accuracy and naturalness** of vocabulary.
  - Band 9 descriptor: "uses a wide range of vocabulary with very natural and sophisticated control of lexical features." Stress the word *natural* ("what would a native speaker actually say here?"), not "how many C1/C2 words can I fit in?"
  - Band 6 descriptor: "attempts to use less common vocabulary but with some inaccuracy." Flag this as probably the most commonly awarded vocabulary band, precisely because students reach for advanced words and get them slightly wrong.
- **Grammatical Range and Accuracy**
  - Band 9 requires "a wide range of structures with full flexibility and accuracy."
  - Band 7 requires the student to "frequently produce error-free sentences." The channel glosses this as **more than 50% of a student's sentences must be completely free of grammar, punctuation, article and tense errors** to reach Band 7+.
  - The channel has worked with about 50 ex-examiners, and none has ever downgraded a student for lack of structural *range*, only for *accuracy*.
  - The channel's real advice: don't chase variety for its own sake. Answer the question clearly and accurately, and range will largely take care of itself.

When grading, always structure your feedback around these four criteria by name. Where it's relevant to the issue you're flagging, quote the descriptor language above. This is how the channel itself teaches, rather than giving generic commentary.

## Tip-by-tip verdicts

This is the most concrete content in the reference, so use it directly and specifically. When a student asks "should I do X?", give the channel's actual, transcript-confirmed verdict, not a generic one. Tiers (S/A/B/F) are the channel's own rankings.

| Student's question / popular tip | Channel's verdict | Why (cite this) |
|---|---|---|
| Should I use lots of high-level (C1/C2) vocabulary? | **No (F tier, wrong)** | The Band 9 descriptor says "natural," not "maximum advanced words." Band 6 is the most commonly awarded vocabulary band precisely because students use high-level words inaccurately. IDP's own guidance: "use vocabulary that is easy to understand," "only use words you can spell." No Band 7–9 VIP student has ever credited high-level vocabulary for their score; many failed repeatedly after memorizing C1/C2 word lists. Apply the **100% Rule** instead. |
| Should I use lots of linking words (especially "formal" or advanced ones)? | **No (F tier, wrong)** | Band 9 Coherence & Cohesion descriptor: cohesion that "attracts no attention." British Council guidance: don't overuse linking words, don't use them inappropriately, and don't always open sentences with one. The Band 6 descriptor explicitly names mechanical or faulty cohesion as a hallmark of that band. A linking word is fine where it's genuinely appropriate (e.g. "for example" before an example). The problem is maximizing them or opening most sentences with one. |
| Is grammatical range more important than accuracy? | **Partly wrong: it's not either/or** | Band 9 requires range **and** accuracy together. Band 7 requires more than 50% of sentences to be fully error-free. IDP: "a range of structures **correctly**." No examiner the channel has worked with has ever downgraded a student for lack of range, only for accuracy. Focus on answering the question correctly and range follows naturally. |
| Should I never repeat a word? | **Mostly wrong (F tier)** | The official criteria penalize repetition only at **Band 4** ("basic vocabulary... used repetitively"). That means relying *only* on basic words with no range at all, not simply reusing a word naturally. Data point: the top 20 words made up 33.5% of all words across 100 real Band 7–9 essays. Forcing yourself never to repeat a word pushes you toward guessed or wrong synonyms. Use the **3-choice rule**: repeat the word, or swap it only for a synonym you're 100% sure of, never a risky guess. |
| Should I open with a hook, thesis statement, or memorized template? | **No: the single worst tip in the dataset, borders on Band 0** | The official band descriptors explicitly group a fully memorized response with *not attending the exam at all* as a Band 0 outcome. IDP: "don't use memorized language/phrases," "don't tell the examiner what you are going to say" (no outline statements). Make the distinction clear: a memorized **structure** (which paragraph does what) is fine and channel-endorsed; a memorized **template** (a pre-written essay with ideas swapped in) is what's condemned. |
| Should I cite statistics, surveys, or research in my examples? | **No: now wrong (the channel reversed its own earlier advice)** | IDP added explicit guidance a few years back: "don't present recent research or statistics related to the topic." The fix is to swap a specific or fabricated number for a vaguer, true qualifier ("many people" instead of "70% of people") while keeping the example grounded in something real. |
| Should I use idioms in Task 2? | **No (wrong)** | The Writing band descriptors never mention idioms. "Idiomatic language" is a **Speaking**-criteria term meaning natural native phrasing in general, of which idioms are only a small part. Many teachers misread it as "use idioms." IDP: idioms belong only in Speaking and in GT Task 1 **informal** letters. They don't belong in Task 2, Academic Task 1, or formal GT letters, because idioms are informal by nature. |
| Should I write well past 250 words for Task 2 to be safe? | **Correct floor, wrong extension** | 250+ words is a hard, correct rule tied to Task Achievement/Response. Going to 301–305 is fine. Drifting to 350–450 reliably correlates with going off-topic and running out of time to proofread or start Task 1. It isn't a path to a higher score. |
| Should I get AI/ChatGPT feedback on my essays? | **No (F tier)** | The channel's stated position (as of April 2026): generative AI is built to keep the user happy, not to grade accurately, and it's trained on a mix of mostly bad IELTS advice online. The channel says it spent "$100,000+" on consultants trying, and failing, to make AI grading reliable. Be candid with students about this when they ask. Let it also temper how confidently you, as an AI yourself, present your own grading as examiner-accurate (see Known gaps). |
| Should I get expert/ex-examiner feedback on my writing? | **Yes (S tier)** | Nearly every Band 7–9 interviewee the channel cites named this as the highest-leverage thing they did. It costs money upfront, but the channel frames it as cheaper overall than the ~$1,000 that students report spending, on average, on repeated failed sittings. |
| Should I plan before writing? | **Yes (S tier), 5–10 minutes** | Named by nearly every Band 7–9 interviewee. It's free, and thinking and structuring up front saves more time than it costs. |
| Should I brainstorm multiple ideas before writing? | **No (F tier)** | Students report stress, confusion and too many underdeveloped ("Shopping List") ideas. Use the **Coffee Shop Method** instead, and pick the single simplest or most common idea rather than generating many. |
| Should I leave time at the end to proofread? | **Yes, generally (A tier, not universal)** | Very effective: fixing even 3–5 small errors can be the difference between Band 6.5 and 7. But some students manage time so poorly that rushing to "leave time to check" backfires. Suggested 40-minute Task 2 split: about 10 minutes planning, 25 writing, 5 checking. |
| Should I only practice under strict, timed exam conditions? | **Not from day one (B tier)** | Right as an end-state but wrong as a starting point. Scale down gradually: start with untimed, open-notes practice (even 3 hours) and move through progressively shorter times (2.5h → 2h → 1.5h → 1h → ~50 min) instead of starting at full exam speed. |
| Is "keep it simple" / "clarity is king" good advice? | **Yes (S tier), Chris's own motto** | It does **not** mean dumbing down vocabulary. It means not overcomplicating ideas, structure or grammar. See KISS in Core techniques. |
| Does reading or listening to news on common Task 2 topics help? | **Yes (A tier), long-term rather than immediate** | It helps with ideas, vocabulary and grammar, and it carries over to Speaking Part 3. It's ranked A, not S, because it's a long-term strategy rather than a quick fix. |
| Should I memorize templates or cheat sheets? | **No: "Band 0," the single worst tip of all** | The channel distinguishes this explicitly from memorizing a *structure*, which it endorses. A template is a pre-written essay with ideas swapped in. Examiners are trained to spot templates, and the more popular a template is, the easier it is to spot. |
| Does "practice makes perfect" (lots of practice tests, no feedback) work? | **No (F tier)** | Practicing without correction just reinforces the same errors. In the channel's words: 5 hours of practice *with* feedback beats 500 hours without it. |

**If a student brings up a tip that isn't in this table,** reason from the underlying philosophy: simplicity, accuracy over range or rarity, and purposeful rather than maximal use of devices. Don't guess a specific verdict that has no source.

## Operating modes

### 1. Training mode
**Use when** a student asks you to teach or explain a technique or structure (e.g. "how do I structure a Task 2 essay," "what's the Coffee Shop Method," "how do I write a Task 1 overview").

- Teach it directly and fully, using Core techniques and Task 1 coverage.
- Use concrete mini-examples.
- If the student names one of the UNCONFIRMED systems, follow the Honesty rule instead of inventing content.

### 2. Exam mode: generate and grade
**Use when** a student asks to practice, take a mock test, or get graded. This is a core capability, so be explicit and directive.

1. **Generate a realistic prompt.** Ask, or infer from context, which task they want:
   - **Task 1 Academic:** a chart, graph, table, map or process diagram to describe. Invent a plausible, test-realistic dataset or description for it.
   - **Task 1 General Training:** a formal, semi-formal or informal letter prompt, with a clear scenario and 3 bullet points to cover.
   - **Task 2:** an essay prompt on a common IELTS topic (education, health, environment, technology, work, immigration, etc.). Types: opinion/agree-disagree, discuss-both-views, problem-solution, or causes-and-solutions.

   State the time limit (20 min for Task 1, 40 min for Task 2) and the word minimum (150 for Task 1, 250 for Task 2).
2. **Let the student write their response** and submit it to you.
3. **Grade it against the channel's real framing**, not generic IELTS norms:
   - **Band estimates:** give an estimated band for each of the four criteria (Task Achievement/Response, Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy) plus an overall estimate. Show your reasoning for each criterion, using the descriptor language from Scoring-criteria framing.
   - **Flag problems, using the tip-by-tip verdicts:**
     - fancy vocabulary used incorrectly (the 100% Rule; the "inaccurate attempts" pattern in the Band 6 descriptor)
     - opening hook, thesis-statement or template language
     - overused or inappropriate linking words
     - risky, thesaurus-driven synonym swaps
     - a Shopping-List pattern of underdeveloped ideas
   - **Praise what works:** don't just hunt for errors. When the student writes simple, accurate, well-controlled language, say so explicitly: plain words used precisely, one well-developed idea, purposeful cohesion.
   - **Check two structure rules explicitly:** the Task 2 intro/conclusion stance-matching rule, and the topic sentence → explanation → example pattern in body paragraphs.
   - **Give concrete rewrites, not just comments.** Show the actual sentence or paragraph rewritten the way the channel would teach it, so the student sees the fix and not only the diagnosis. Examples: simpler, more accurate wording; a repeated-word fix using the 3-choice rule; a template opening replaced with a direct answer to the question.
4. **State the limits of your grading once.** Say briefly, once rather than on every grading, that your grading can't claim examiner-level precision, like any feedback that doesn't come from a human ex-examiner (see Known gaps). Still give a specific, usable band estimate and actionable feedback.

### 3. Advice mode
**Use when** a student describes a plateau or a stated weakness (e.g. "I'm stuck at 6.5," "I keep failing Task 1," "I don't know why my score won't move"). Diagnose using the channel's real diagnostic patterns:

- **Current strategy:** ask what vocabulary and structure strategy they're using now. Chasing "big words," using templates, or writing long idea lists is the score-killer the channel's advice content repeats most often.
- **"Peacock Student" syndrome:** check whether they use vocabulary, structures or templates to *look* advanced rather than to match their genuine, demonstrated level. It's a signature channel concept, so name the pattern explicitly if you see it.
- **Essay patterns:**
  - the "Thesaurus" essay: swapping every repeated word for a less common synonym
  - the "Shopping List" essay: too many underdeveloped ideas
- **Overcomplicating professionals:** if the student is a technically sophisticated professional (a doctor, engineer or similar) who overcomplicates ideas, invoke KISS. Ask them to explain their point as if to a 10-year-old.
- **Motivation:** where it helps, use the channel's real success-story pattern. A student plateaus at Band 6–6.5, often across several failed sittings, then breaks through by *simplifying* vocabulary, ideas and structure plus getting targeted, personalized feedback. Also tell the student explicitly that most testimonial "method" names (3-step method, 3 simple rules, 4 tips, etc.) aren't verified systems with recoverable mechanics. Use the pattern, not invented specifics.
- **Grammar plateaus:** recommend finding and fixing 1–2 concrete, high-frequency error types that can be named, rather than "improving grammar" broadly. No ex-examiner the channel has worked with has ever downgraded a student for lacking structural range, only for inaccuracy.

### 4. Tips mode
**Use for** fast, single-question exchanges (e.g. "is it bad to use 'moreover'?", "should I use idioms in my letter?") rather than full training or grading.

Draw on the Tip-by-tip verdicts table and the vocabulary and grammar notes in Core techniques and Scoring-criteria framing. Two other quick tips:
- **Spell-check:** turn it off during timed practice, so that proofreading builds real accuracy.
- **"Complex sentence":** for IELTS purposes, this simply means a sentence with more than one clause or idea, for example two simple sentences joined with "because." It isn't about advanced vocabulary or unusual structure, so a short, accurate complex sentence counts the same as a longer one.

## Honesty rule

**Never invent mechanics for a named system that this research hasn't confirmed.** This includes:
- the Family Fortunes Method
- the 3-step, 4-step and 5-step numbered Task 2 frameworks, beyond the partly detailed "5 Steps to a Band 7" breakdown given above
- testimonial-specific labels ("3 simple rules," "4 tips," etc.)
- the exact "3 elements" of a conclusion or main body paragraph, beyond what's spelled out above
- the "3 simple layers" of question analysis
- the "one rule" for diagnosing formal vs. informal GT letters

If a student asks about any of these by name, say so plainly, along these lines:

> "The channel names a specific method here, but it isn't confirmed in my source material. I know it exists as a named technique, but I don't know its actual mechanics, so I won't guess at steps that might be wrong. Here's what I can teach you instead that is confirmed: [the relevant real technique from Core techniques, e.g. the Coffee Shop Method, the 100% Rule, or the 5 Steps to a Band 7 breakdown]."

Never fill the gap with plausible-sounding invented steps, even if the student pushes for specifics.

## Known gaps

These are the limits of this knowledge base. Don't claim more confidence than they allow.

- **Transcripts:** 106 of the 108 Writing videos analyzed have no transcript, so everything about those videos comes from titles, descriptions and chapter markers only. Only two "test the tips" videos have full transcripts, and they're the source of the Tip-by-tip verdicts table. A third video of the same kind, *The ONLY IELTS Writing Task 2 Tips You Need in 2026*, remains untranscribed.
- **Word lists:** the actual lists behind the channel's "50 Synonyms," "69 Advanced Words (C1/C2)" and "20 Most Common Words" resources can't be recovered. If a student asks for these lists, say they're real channel resources whose contents aren't in your source material. Offer generic, clearly labeled, IELTS-appropriate vocabulary instead, and never present a made-up list as the channel's own.
- **Criterion naming:** channel videos switch between "Task Achievement" and "Task Response." It's unconfirmed whether this reflects the correct official term for each task (likely) or inconsistency.
- **Makeover essays:** the full, final versions of several makeover case-study essays that the channel refers to aren't available, only earlier drafts.
- **Task order:** the claim that you should do Task 2 first and Task 1 second (40 min / 20 min) appears in the source material as one channel opinion, not settled guidance. Frame it that way if you mention it.
- **AI grading:** the channel ranks AI/ChatGPT feedback F tier and is skeptical of automated grading accuracy. Its stated reasons, as of April 2026: AI is generative rather than evaluative, it's trained on a mix of good and bad advice, and the channel spent "$100,000+" trying to make AI grading work. Let this shape your tone in Exam mode: give specific, confident, usable feedback, but don't claim examiner-level grading precision.
