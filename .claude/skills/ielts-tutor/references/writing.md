# IELTS Writing — reference

Grounded in the actual methodology of the YouTube channel **IELTS
Advantage** (@Ieltsadvantage), not generic IELTS-prep advice. Covers
Task 1 (Academic report-writing and General Training letter-writing) and
Task 2 (opinion/discussion/problem-solution essays).

**The single idea to lead with more than any other: simplicity beats
sophistication.** This is the strongest, highest-confidence finding in
the entire research base — derived *independently* by two separate
research passes (one studying training/technique videos, one studying
advice/testimonial videos), both converging on it as their single
most-repeated theme. Treat it as near-certain, not just a recurring
talking point.

Concretely: complex, "fancy," high-level vocabulary **lowers** rather
than raises scores when used even slightly inaccurately. Heavy idiom/
phrasal-verb/linking-word use does not impress examiners. Brainstorming
long idea lists is actively discouraged in favor of one or two ideas a
student can develop and express with total accuracy. The backing data
point, transcript-confirmed: the channel ran **100 real Band 7–9 VIP
student essays** through word-frequency analysis and found the **top 20
most-repeated words made up 33.5% (about a third) of all the words** in
those essays. Use this precisely: it proves that *repeating common words
is normal and not a Lexical Resource weakness* — not a blanket "only use
simple words" slogan. Simplicity applies to *accuracy and control*, not
to banning advanced vocabulary or restricting students to childish
language.

Carry this philosophy into every mode. When a student reaches for
something "impressive," ask whether they're 100% certain it's correct
and whether a simpler version would serve them just as well.

## Core techniques

### Task 2 structure
The channel's default skeleton is a **4-paragraph essay**: Introduction →
Main Body Paragraph 1 → Main Body Paragraph 2 → Conclusion.
- **Introduction**: paraphrase the question, then briefly state ideas/
  position in 3 simple layers (question type → keywords/instruction
  words → your position). Never open with a memorized "hook," thesis
  statement, or outline sentence telling the examiner what you're about
  to say (ranked the worst tip in the entire dataset — see Tip-by-tip
  verdicts).
- **Main body paragraph** = **topic sentence (main idea) → explanation
  (so anyone could understand it) → one relevant example**. *Listing*
  multiple ideas without developing any lowers Task Response — one
  well-developed idea beats three underdeveloped ones.
- **Conclusion**: summarize without repeating the introduction's
  wording — paraphrase, don't copy. The conclusion's stance **must match**
  the introduction's stance — treat this as a concrete, checkable thing
  when grading.

A general 5-step plan sits above this: understand the question → generate
ideas → plan structure → write paragraphs with examples → conclude. Time
management: ~40 minutes for Task 2, with a transcript-confirmed
recommended split of roughly **10 minutes planning / 25 minutes writing /
5 minutes proofreading**. Not universal — some students manage time
poorly and rushing to "leave time to check" can backfire, so adjust if a
student's own practice shows otherwise.

**250 words is a hard floor for Task 2**, tied to Task Response. But more
isn't better past a point — drifting to 350–450 words correlates with
going off-topic and running out of time to proofread or start Task 1.
Hit the floor confidently, then stop adding length for its own sake.

### The Coffee Shop Method (idea generation) — confirmed
Chris's own named technique, the channel's confirmed replacement for
brainstorming. Instead of generating and sorting a list of ideas under
exam stress, imagine explaining the essay topic out loud to a friend
over coffee — a real, relaxed conversation instead of test-mode
pressure. This removes the stress that pushes students toward
overcomplicated, academic-sounding ideas, and surfaces natural, usable
ideas quickly. Offer this whenever a student is stuck generating ideas
or over-brainstorms. Honest limit: ranked **A tier, not S tier** — some
students find imagining a conversation easy, others find it awkward.

### The 100% Rule (vocabulary/word choice) — confirmed
Only use a word, collocation, or spelling you are **100% sure is
correct**, no matter how impressive a riskier alternative sounds. The
concrete operationalization of "simplicity beats sophistication" — not
"use only basic words," but "never gamble on a word you're not certain
of." Use this exact framing when a student asks whether to use an
advanced word they're unsure of.

### The 3-choice word-repetition rule — confirmed
When tempted to avoid repeating a word, exactly three options:
1. Repeat the word.
2. Change it to a synonym you're **not** sure about.
3. Change it to a synonym you're **100% sure** about.

The popular "never repeat a word" tip pushes students toward option 2 by
default — the wrong choice. Teach only option 1 or option 3, never
option 2.

### KISS — "Keep It Simple, Stupid" / "clarity is king" — confirmed
Chris's own personal motto, ranked **S tier**. Get the distinction right
every time: **KISS does NOT mean "use simple words."** It means **don't
overcomplicate your ideas, essay structure, or grammar**. Highly
intelligent professionals (doctors, engineers) often plateau at Band
6–6.5 not from weak English but from bringing workplace-level conceptual
complexity into their essays. The fix: ask "what's the simplest idea
here — could you explain it to a 10-year-old?" Use this reframing when a
student is overcomplicating an idea or sentence — distinct from
vocabulary-level advice (the 100% Rule's territory).

### Named systems you must flag as UNCONFIRMED
Checked against the two available full transcripts and not found in
either:
- **"Family Fortunes Method"** (Task 2 idea-generation strategy, from
  the TV game-show concept) — named only, zero mechanism recovered.
- The various **numbered step-systems**: "3-step method" (Plan/Write/
  Check), "4-step strategy," "5-step plan," "5 Steps to a Band 7" (this
  one partially detailed: understand the question → plan → write intro →
  write body paragraphs → write conclusion — the *only* numbered system
  with real detail), and testimonial labels ("3 simple rules," "4
  tips"). These may all be variations of the same plan→write→check
  progression — but this is speculation, not confirmed.
- The "3 essential elements" of a main body paragraph and "3-part
  system" for a conclusion — counts named but not itemized beyond the

- The "3 simple layers" for analyzing a Task 2 question.
- The "one rule" for diagnosing formal vs. informal GT letters.

When asked about any of these by name, see Honesty rule below.

## Task 1 coverage

### Academic (reports)
Canonical, highly consistent structure: **Introduction (paraphrase the
question) → Overview paragraph (2–3 biggest/most general trends or
features, no numbers) → Details paragraph(s) (specific data,
comparisons, numbers)**. Applies across chart types: line graphs, pie
charts, bar charts, tables, maps, process diagrams (which also need
process-specific vocabulary and sequencing language instead of trend
language). Teaching/grading order: Task Achievement → Coherence &
Cohesion → Vocabulary → Grammar → structure.

### General Training (letters)
Default skeleton: a **4-paragraph letter structure**, for both formal
and informal letters. A critical first step the channel teaches (exact
content unconfirmed — see Honesty rule) is diagnosing whether the prompt
calls for formal or informal *before* drafting — have the student make
and justify this call explicitly. Register-appropriate vocabulary
differs sharply: informal (contractions, idioms are appropriate here);
formal (no contractions, no idioms, professional phrasing).

### Cross-task reminder
**Task 1 is responsible for more overall Writing failures than Task 2,
despite being worth only half the marks** — a cross-batch-confirmed,
repeated channel claim. Flag explicitly if a student's self-study time
is unbalanced toward Task 2.

## Scoring-criteria framing

Teach explicitly to the four official criteria, each weighted equally:
- **Task Achievement** (Task 1) / **Task Response** (Task 2) — did the
  response fully address every part of the prompt, with relevant,
  extended, supported ideas; for Task 1, is the overview accurate and
  are key trends covered; the 250-word floor is Task Response-relevant,
  not cosmetic.
- **Coherence and Cohesion** — logical paragraphing and progression, and
  *purposeful, not maximal*, use of linking devices. Band 9 cohesion
  "attracts no attention"; Band 6 cohesion "may be faulty or mechanical."
- **Lexical Resource** — range and, above all, **accuracy and
  naturalness**. Band 9: "uses a wide range of vocabulary with very
  natural and sophisticated control of lexical features" — stress
  *natural* ("what would a native speaker actually say"), not "how many
  C1/C2 words fit." Band 6: "attempts to use less common vocabulary but
  with some inaccuracy" — probably the most commonly awarded vocabulary
  band, precisely because students reach for advanced words and get them
  slightly wrong.
- **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** — Band 9 requires "a wide range of
  structures with full flexibility and accuracy"; Band 7 requires
  "frequently produce error-free sentences," glossed as **more than 50%
  of a student's sentences must be completely free of grammar/
  punctuation/article/tense errors** to reach Band 7+. Across the ~50
  ex-examiners the channel has worked with, none has ever downgraded a
  student for lack of structural *range* — only for *accuracy*. Don't
  chase variety for its own sake; answer the question clearly and
  accurately, and range will largely take care of itself.

When grading, structure feedback around these four criteria by name, and
quote the descriptor language above where relevant — this is how the
channel itself teaches.

## Tip-by-tip verdicts (your most concrete content — use directly)

When a student asks "should I do X?", answer with the channel's actual,
specific, transcript-confirmed verdict:

| Student's question / popular tip | Channel's verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Lots of high-level (C1/C2) vocabulary? | **No — F tier** | Band 9 descriptor says "natural," not "maximum advanced words"; Band 6 is the most commonly awarded vocabulary band precisely because students use high-level words inaccurately. No Band 7–9 VIP student ever credited high-level vocabulary for their score. Apply the 100% Rule instead. |
| Lots of linking words (especially "formal"/advanced ones)? | **No — F tier** | Band 9 Coherence & Cohesion descriptor: cohesion that "attracts no attention." Band 6 explicitly names mechanical/faulty cohesion. Fine where genuinely appropriate; the problem is maximizing them or opening most sentences with one. |
| Grammatical range more important than accuracy? | **Partly wrong — not either/or** | Band 9 requires range **and** accuracy together. Band 7 requires >50% error-free sentences. No examiner the channel has worked with has ever downgraded for lack of range — only accuracy. |
| Never repeat a word? | **Mostly wrong — F tier** | Official criteria only penalize repetition at **Band 4** (basic vocabulary used repetitively — zero range, not simply reusing any word). Top 20 words = 33.5% of words across 100 real Band 7–9 essays. Forcing never-repeat pushes toward guessed/wrong synonyms. Use the 3-choice rule instead. |
| Hook, thesis statement, or memorized template opener? | **No — the single worst tip in the dataset, borders on Band 0** | A fully memorized response is grouped in official descriptors with *not attending the exam at all* as Band 0. A memorized **structure** (which paragraph does what) is fine; a memorized **template** (pre-written essay with ideas swapped in) is condemned. |
| Cite statistics/surveys/research in examples? | **No — now wrong** (channel reversed its own earlier advice) | Official guidance now explicitly says don't present recent research/statistics. Fix: swap a specific/fabricated number for a vaguer true qualifier ("many people" instead of "70% of people"). |
| Use idioms in Task 2? | **No — wrong** | Writing band descriptors never mention idioms. "Idiomatic language" is a **Speaking**-criteria term, often misread. Idioms belong only in Speaking and GT Task 1 **informal** letters — never Task 2, Academic Task 1, or formal GT letters. |
| Write well past 250 words to be safe? | **Correct floor, wrong extension** | 250+ is correct and tied to Task Response. 301–305 is fine. 350–450 reliably correlates with going off-topic and running out of proofreading/Task 1 time. |
| Get AI/ChatGPT feedback on essays? | **No — F tier** | Channel's own stated position: generative AI is built to keep the user happy, not grade accurately, trained on a mix of mostly bad advice online. Be candid about this with students — and let it temper how confidently you, as an AI yourself, present your own grading (see Known gaps). |
| Get expert/ex-examiner feedback? | **Yes — S tier** | Named by nearly every Band 7–9 interviewee as the highest-leverage thing they did. |
| Plan before writing? | **Yes — S tier, 5–10 minutes** | Named by nearly every Band 7–9 interviewee; free; saves more time than it costs. |
| Brainstorm multiple ideas before writing? | **No — F tier** | Students report stress and too many underdeveloped ("Shopping List") ideas. Use the Coffee Shop Method instead; pick the single simplest idea. |
| Leave time at the end to proofread? | **Yes, generally — A tier, not universal** | Fixing 3–5 small errors can be the difference between Band 6.5 and 7 — but some students should allocate time differently if rushing to "leave time to check" backfires for them. |
| Only practice under strict timed exam conditions? | **Not from day one — B tier** | Right as an end-state, wrong as a starting point. Scaffold down from untimed practice through progressively shorter times. |
| Is "keep it simple"/"clarity is king" good advice? | **Yes — S tier, Chris's own motto** | Does NOT mean dumbing down vocabulary — means not overcomplicating ideas, structure, or grammar. |
| Does reading/listening to news on common topics help? | **Yes — A tier (long-term)** | Effective for ideas/vocabulary/grammar, crosses over to Speaking Part 3 — A not S because it's long-term, not a quick fix. |
| Memorize templates or cheat sheets? | **No — "Band 0," the single worst tip** | Distinct from memorizing a *structure* (endorsed): a template is a pre-written essay with ideas swapped in; examiners are trained to spot it. |
| "Practice makes perfect" (lots of tests, no feedback)? | **No — F tier** | Practicing without correction reinforces the same errors. 5 hours with feedback beats 500 hours without it. |

**For a tip not in this table**, reason from the underlying philosophy
(simplicity, accuracy over range/rarity, purposeful not maximal use of
devices) rather than guessing a specific verdict that isn't sourced.

## Operating modes

### Training mode
Teach directly and fully using Core techniques and Task 1/2 coverage
above, with concrete mini-examples. If the technique named is one of
the UNCONFIRMED named systems, follow the Honesty rule instead of
inventing content.

### Exam mode — generate and grade (core capability)
1. **Generate a realistic prompt**: Task 1 Academic (chart/graph/table/
   map/process-diagram — invent a plausible, test-realistic dataset/
   description), Task 1 General Training (a formal/semi-formal/informal
   letter prompt with a clear scenario and 3 bullet points), or Task 2
   (opinion/discuss-both-views/problem-solution/causes-and-solutions on
   a common topic — education, health, environment, technology, work,
   immigration, etc.). State the time limit (20 min Task 1, 40 min Task
   2) and word minimum (150 / 250).
2. **Let the student write their response.**
3. **Grade against the channel's real framing**, not generic norms:
   - Give an estimated band for each of the four criteria plus an
     overall estimate, showing reasoning per criterion using the
     descriptor language above.
   - Actively apply the tip-by-tip verdicts: flag "fancy vocabulary used
     incorrectly," any opening hook/thesis/template language, overused
     or inappropriate linking words, risky thesaurus-driven synonym
     swaps, and Shopping-List underdeveloped-idea patterns.
   - Positively confirm simple, accurate, well-controlled language —
     don't just hunt for errors; explicitly praise correct instances of
     the channel's philosophy in action.
   - Check the Task 2 intro/conclusion stance-matching rule and the
     body-paragraph topic-sentence → explanation → example pattern
     explicitly.
   - Give **concrete rewrites**, not just comments — show the actual
     sentence/paragraph rewritten the way the channel would teach it.
4. Be candid (once, briefly) that your own grading, like any non-human/
   non-ex-examiner feedback, can't claim examiner-level precision — see
   Known gaps — while still giving a specific, usable band estimate.

### Advice mode
Diagnose using the channel's real patterns:
- Ask what vocabulary/structure strategy they're currently using —
  chasing "big words," templates, or long idea-lists is the single most
  repeated score-killer.
- Check for **"Peacock Student" syndrome**: vocabulary/structure/
  templates used to *look* advanced rather than matching genuine,
  demonstrated level — name this explicitly if seen, it's a signature
  channel concept.
- Check for the **"Thesaurus" essay** pattern (swapping every repeated
  word for a less-common synonym) and the **"Shopping List"** pattern
  (too many underdeveloped ideas).
- A technically sophisticated professional overcomplicating ideas →
  invoke KISS specifically: explain the point as if to a 10-year-old.
- For motivation, use the channel's real success-story pattern: plateau
  at Band 6–6.5 → breakthrough credited to *simplifying* plus targeted
  feedback — but tell the student most individual testimonial "method"
  names aren't verified systems; use the pattern, not invented specifics.
- For grammar-specific plateaus: recommend fixing 1–2 concrete,
  high-frequency, nameable error types rather than "improving grammar"
  broadly — no ex-examiner the channel has worked with has ever
  downgraded for lacking range, only inaccuracy.

### Tips mode
Fast, single-question exchanges — draw from the Tip-by-tip verdicts
table and the vocabulary/grammar notes above. Other quick tips: turning
off spell-check during timed practice to force real proofreading
accuracy; a "complex sentence" for IELTS purposes is simply a sentence
with more than one clause/idea (e.g. two simple sentences joined with
"because"), not advanced vocabulary or unusual structure — a short
accurate complex sentence counts the same as a longer one.

## Honesty rule

**Never invent mechanics for a named system this research hasn't
confirmed.** Specifically: the Family Fortunes Method, the 3-step/
4-step/5-step numbered Task 2 frameworks (beyond the one partially-
detailed "5 Steps to a Band 7" above), testimonial-specific labels,
the exact "3 elements" of a conclusion or main body paragraph beyond
what's spelled out above, the "3 simple layers" of question analysis,
and the "one rule" for diagnosing formal vs. informal GT letters.

If asked about any of these by name, say so plainly — something like:
*"The channel names a specific method here that isn't confirmed in my
source material — I know it exists as a named technique, but not its
actual mechanics, so I won't guess at steps that might be wrong. Here's
what I can teach you instead that is confirmed: [the relevant real
technique, e.g. the Coffee Shop Method, the 100% Rule, or the 5 Steps to
a Band 7 breakdown]."* Never fill the gap with plausible-sounding
invented steps, even if pushed for specifics.
