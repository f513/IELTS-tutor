# Speaking — EXAM Subtype Analysis (Partial Batch: 83 of 179 Speaking videos)

Source: `data/analysis_input/speaking_exam.md` (83 videos, titles + descriptions + durations only — **no transcripts available** for any video in this batch; YouTube currently blocks transcript downloads). This is the exam-subtype slice only; training/advice and tips subtypes are covered in separate batches and will be merged later.

## 1. Overview

This batch is overwhelmingly **mock/real IELTS Speaking test footage** — students (mostly the channel's own tutoring clients) taking a simulated speaking exam, usually followed by scored feedback from the channel's host (Chris Pell, "IELTS Advantage"). It splits cleanly into two structural buckets by duration:

- **Short-form clips ("#shorts", 14s–1:16)** — roughly half the batch (~45 videos). These show a single Part 1 question/answer, a single comparison, or a short "guess the score" hook. No full-test structure, no detailed scored feedback — just a label (band score) and sometimes one line of commentary in the title/description.
- **Long-form full mock tests (7:31–30:51)** — roughly the other half (~38 videos). These explicitly walk through a complete IELTS Speaking test (Part 1, Part 2 cue card, Part 3) followed by detailed examiner-style feedback and a band score, sometimes broken down against the 4 official IELTS marking criteria.

Band scores dominate the naming convention — nearly every title/description states a specific band (5, 6.5, 7, 7.5, 8, 8.5, 9) or a comparison between two bands, confirming this subtype is explicitly built around **"what does Band X actually sound/look like."**

Two recurring sub-formats within exam content:
- **Single-student mock test + feedback** (most common for long-form).
- **Paired/compared students** answering the *same* question, with the viewer asked to guess/judge who scored higher (common for shorts: "Compare These IELTS Speaking Answers: Who Wins?", "Which Student Should Get A Band 9?", "Which Student Will Get A Higher IELTS Score?").

A smaller but notable set are **progress/before-after** videos showing one student's test twice (e.g., "She Raised Her IELTS Speaking 1.5 Bands in 1 Week" — explicitly "two full speaking tests — before and after coaching").

## 4. What "good" vs "weak" answers look like per the descriptions

Recurring stated criteria/phrases (channel-specific framing, not generic IELTS text):
- **"Natural vocabulary" / "topic-specific vocabulary beats memorized word lists"** — stated explicitly in "IELTS Speaking Test – Perfect Band 9" (2026): "Why using topic-specific vocabulary beats memorized word lists."
- **Enthusiasm/informality as a positive signal**: "IELTS Speaking Test Band 8.5- Almost Perfect" (2026) states "Informal language and emotion help your pronunciation score" and "why being enthusiastic and natural beats memorised answers."
- **Fluency fixes named repeatedly**: "reducing pauses," "speaking for the full two minutes," "using more intonation and connected speech" ("IELTS Speaking Practice Test- Almost Perfect," Band 8.5).
- **Answer length/development**: "developing ideas fully," "using the cue card effectively," "balancing personal views with broader perspectives in Part 3" ("IELTS Speaking Test with Feedback – Band 8.5," 2024).
- **Real-life/personal examples boost score**: "How using real-life examples from work can boost your score" (Band 7.5 test).
- **Small/tiny mistakes framing for near-miss bands**: "small but important errors that can lower your score" (Band 8.0 practice exam); "the tiny mistakes that kept her from Band 9" (Band 8.5 test, 2026); "the one thing holding her back" (Perfect Band 9 test, 2026 family-gatherings video).
- **Grammar-specific diagnostic**: "Why articles are the #1 grammar problem for Russian speakers" ("Why She Was Stuck at 6.5").
- **Older evergreen explicit framework** (from the two 2019 Rashmi videos, which explicitly cite the 4 IELTS criteria): don't memorize answers, don't give too-short Part 1 answers, don't over-extend Part 1 answers (aim 2-3 sentences), relax and talk like a real person, idioms only help if used correctly, accent/body language are NOT scored.
- **Weak-answer markers** (inferred from "stuck at" video titles): freezing/going blank on Part 3 ("She Raised Her IELTS Speaking 1.5 Bands in 1 Week" — "why she was freezing on Part 3 questions"), overthinking ("avoid overthinking" — Band 8 "stuck" video), simple/plain vocabulary needing upgrade ("IELTS Speaking Feedback - Band 6 to 9 Vocabulary" — "help this student improve her simple vocabulary").

Fluency/Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy, Pronunciation are named as the explicit 4-criterion rubric only in the two oldest long-form videos (2019 Rashmi tests); newer videos describe feedback more loosely ("honest feedback," "expert feedback," "tips to improve") without naming all 4 criteria every time — so the explicit rubric-citation habit may have faded in the channel's more recent (2024–2026) content, based on description language alone.

## 6. Common mistakes/feedback themes (recurring "what this student did wrong/right")

- Pauses / insufficient fluency — needing to "reduce pauses" and "speak for the full two minutes."
- Memorized-sounding answers vs. natural/spontaneous answers — repeatedly contrasted as the key differentiator near the top bands.
- Simple/generic vocabulary vs. topic-specific vocabulary — named explicitly as a mistake-to-fix pattern across several videos (Band 6-to-9 vocabulary video, Band 9 "topic-specific vocabulary" video).
- Grammar — specific error type named once concretely: article misuse for Russian-speaking students ("Why She Was Stuck at 6.5").
- Under-developed Part 3 answers / not balancing personal opinion with broader perspective.
- Going blank / freezing under pressure, especially in Part 3.
- Small/isolated errors that cap an otherwise strong performance just below Band 9 (the "near-perfect"/"almost perfect" framing used for most 8.5 videos).
- Volume/delivery coaching: "speak louder" listed as a fix in "Fix These 3 Things To Get Band 8."
- Giving examples to extend/develop short answers (recurs across "Fix These 3 Things," the 2019 Rashmi tips, and others).

## 7. Gaps / low-confidence areas

- **No transcripts anywhere in this batch** — we cannot know the actual wording of any student answer, any cue card beyond the one explicitly quoted in the description (Rashmi's clothing cue card), or the examiner's/Chris's exact spoken feedback script. Everything above is inferred from title/description marketing copy, which is written to sell/tease the video and may overstate or compress what's actually said.
- **Band-score accuracy is unverifiable** — we only have the channel's self-reported/claimed band scores; we cannot confirm against transcript evidence that the feedback in-video actually matches IELTS official band descriptors.
- **Title/description mismatches exist** — e.g., "IELTS Speaking Band 6 vs Band 9 Answer" describes "3 common grammar mistakes," not an actual Band 6 vs 9 comparison; treat titles as approximate, not literal, when building training data.
- **No confirmation of examiner identity/credentials** (native speaker status, IELTS-certified examiner vs. coach) — descriptions call Chris "an IELTS expert"/"teacher" but don't claim official examiner certification.
- **No cue cards given for most long-form tests** — only one video explicitly quotes its Part 2 cue card text in full; for the rest we know only the general topic area mentioned in the description, not the exact cue card wording or bullet points.
- **Low-band (5, 6, 7) standalone full tests are absent** — any modeling of those bands from this batch alone would be extrapolation from comparison-video snippets only, not full-test grounding.
- **Duration-to-structure inference is a heuristic, not a confirmed fact** — we're inferring "full 3-part test" from long duration + description language; a few long videos (e.g., "She Raised Her IELTS Speaking 1.5 Bands in 1 Week," 26:06) show *two* tests back to back, so per-test depth may be shorter than duration suggests.

## 8. Agent brief contribution (Speaking specialist agent — EXAM-style content)

1. **[Channel-specific]** When running or scoring a mock speaking test, structure it as three distinct phases matching the channel's template: Part 1 (~4 min, personal/everyday questions: job/study, hometown, family), Part 2 (cue card + 1 min prep + up to 2 min speaking), Part 3 (abstract discussion tied to the Part 2 topic, ~4-5 min) — this timing breakdown is stated explicitly by the channel, not just generic IELTS format.
2. **[Channel-specific]** After the mock test, give feedback organized around the 4 official IELTS Speaking criteria by name — Pronunciation, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, Fluency and Coherence — matching how the channel's own full-feedback videos are structured.
3. **[Channel-specific]** Prize natural, spontaneous, topic-specific vocabulary over memorized phrases/word lists — repeatedly used by the channel as the line between Band 8.5-ish and Band 9 answers; call out answers that sound rehearsed as a specific deduction, not just a vague note.
4. **[Channel-specific]** Treat enthusiasm, informal natural language, and emotional engagement as positively correlated with pronunciation/fluency scores in this channel's framing (not a standard rubric claim — it's the channel's own pedagogical emphasis).
5. **[Channel-specific]** For near-miss high performers (Band 8/8.5 heading toward 9), focus feedback on "small/tiny" fixable errors rather than wholesale rework — the channel's typical framing for this band range.
6. **[Channel-specific]** For Part 1, coach for 2-3 sentence answers with one supporting explanation or example — not single-word/short answers, not over-long rehearsed monologues (explicit tip from two of the long-form videos).
7. **[Channel-specific]** Flag going blank / freezing in Part 3 as a common, specifically named failure mode to coach around (strategies: don't overthink, use a structured response technique) rather than a vocabulary or grammar issue.
8. **[Generic IELTS knowledge, confirmed by channel]** Idioms/phrasal verbs only raise the Lexical Resource score when used correctly and naturally; incorrect use can lower it — don't recommend idiom-stuffing.
9. **[Generic IELTS knowledge, confirmed by channel]** Accent (British/American affectation) and body language/appearance are not assessed criteria — don't coach students toward a fake accent or over-index on posture/eye contact as score factors.
10. **[Channel-specific]** When doing paired/comparison exercises (two students, same question), use it as a diagnostic teaching device to make scoring criteria concrete — this is a recurring, deliberate format in the channel's content, useful for an agent that wants to show contrastive examples at adjacent bands.
11. **[Low-confidence / generic]** Grammar error patterns may be first-language-specific (e.g., article omission for Russian speakers) — an agent should be cautious about generalizing this without more data, since it comes from a single video description, not a transcript-verified pattern across many students.
12. **[Gap to flag to the merge pass]** This batch alone does not give grounded full-test material for Bands 5, 6, or 7 — the merge pass (combining with training/advice and tips batches) should check whether those other subtypes fill in lower-band full-test modeling; if not, the final agent brief should note that low-band mock-test grounding is thin across the whole channel corpus.
