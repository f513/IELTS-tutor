## 2. Techniques & strategies taught

- **Prioritize Parts 3 & 4 in prep time** — recurs across V1Uosk72mQU and lpF5_EZIKVE. Framed as a time-allocation strategy, not a question-answering technique. **Confirmed in q7xCHfDRdug** with the reasoning behind it (see Section 1).
- **Per-question-type strategy (reject universal strategy)** — gvZyZpzifcY ("The WORST IELTS Listening Strategy"); reinforced structurally by the channel having dedicated strategy videos for Maps (-aBW-RHtcrE) and Multiple Choice (vpmJTSntyVw) rather than one general strategy video. **q7xCHfDRdug now gives the actual mechanics for both — see below and Section 3.**
- **Maps/diagram-labelling strategy — mechanics now confirmed via q7xCHfDRdug's transcript** (cross-checked against what -aBW-RHtcrE's description implied; consistent, and now with real procedural detail rather than just a chapter list). Paraphrased from the transcript: (1) read the instructions first to confirm whether you're writing a letter code or an actual place name; (2) study the map before the audio starts — identify every labelled feature and entrance, and mentally trace plausible routes between them; (3) for each answer option, pre-think likely vocabulary (e.g. a "cafe" might be referred to as a "coffee shop") and use real-world logic about where a feature would plausibly sit (toilets/cafes near buildings, not in the middle of open woodland); (4) listen very closely to the opening lines to establish *where the tour starts and which direction it moves* — "turn right" means something different depending on the starting gate, and this is called out as the single biggest practical error; (5) actively visualize walking the route in real time rather than just scanning the map statically — for students who report struggling with this, the channel reframes "I'm bad at visualization" as self-sabotaging talk rather than a fixed trait, and recommends a low-stakes drill of listening *only* to practice visualizing the route, without trying to answer questions yet; (6) watch for the same "changed/revised answer" trap used elsewhere on the test — a location is stated and then revised, moved, closed, or described as "going to be built," and the real answer is the corrected one; (7) use signposting phrases ("let's start at...", "now let's go back to...", "let's finish at...") as navigation anchors; (8) when practicing, deliberately slow down or replay to master the *process* first, only building up to real exam speed once the steps feel automatic — speed is the last thing trained, not the first.
- **Multiple Choice strategy — mechanics now confirmed via q7xCHfDRdug's transcript** (cross-checked against vpmJTSntyVw's description; consistent "new approach to understanding the clip and the options," now with real procedural detail). Paraphrased from the transcript: (1) read instructions carefully; (2) as soon as one question cluster ends, immediately read ahead into the next set of MC questions rather than dwelling on one you just struggled with; (3) identify/underline keywords in both the question stem and each of the answer options; (4) explicitly compare options pairwise to isolate what actually differs between near-identical-looking choices (the transcript's demonstrated example: two options both mention "usefulness," but one compares variations *within* fridges and the other compares fridges *to other appliances* — the real distinction is that comparison axis, not the surface topic); (5) core stated principle — **"it's not a listening test, it's a listening and thinking test"** — don't lock in the first answer-shaped phrase you hear; the correct option is determined by matching to the *specific question asked*, not by recognizing a phrase; (6) treat each question as covering one segment of the audio and withhold your final choice until that whole segment finishes, since speakers often revise themselves mid-thought; (7) listen specifically for contrastive connectors ("but," "however") — the transcript's demonstrated example shows a speaker giving an apparently positive statement that a following "however" then reverses, flipping the correct answer; (8) prediction is optional for MC (unlike sentence completion, below) since all options are often genuinely plausible — don't force it; (9) if stuck, guess decisively and move on immediately so as not to lose the rest of the question cluster.
- **"Perfect practice strategy" (diagnostic practice loop) — new, from q7xCHfDRdug.** This operationalizes the "error-pattern diagnosis" philosophy into a concrete procedure: (1) use only real/official practice tests (see Section 1) under true exam conditions (no pausing, no replaying, no prior familiarity, timed); (2) do this until you score at or above your target band **three times in a row** before ever booking the real test; (3) if you are not hitting that consistently, go to the back of the book, find the audio script, and mark every wrong answer honestly (a misspelling is marked wrong, not "basically right"); (4) categorize *why* each answer was wrong into one of four buckets the transcript names explicitly — spelling, a specific question-type weakness (e.g. consistently losing points on Maps or MC specifically, which is "a strategy issue, not a listening issue"), a focus lapse, or a vocabulary gap; (5) work specifically on that identified weakness before doing more full practice tests — the transcript is explicit that "doing more practice tests will not magically improve your spelling/vocabulary," i.e. generic repetition is explicitly rejected as the fix once a specific weakness is identified.
- **Within-test resilience techniques — new, from q7xCHfDRdug**: (a) "you can get a question wrong and still get a Band 9" — don't let one missed/lost-focus question cascade into panic that costs subsequent questions; (b) "keep moving or you're dead" (the transcript's own analogy, borrowed from close-quarters-combat training) — freezing on a hard question is what causes multi-question losses, not the hard question itself; (c) focus primarily on the current question while keeping peripheral awareness on the *topic cue* that signals the next question is coming (explicitly compared to peripheral attention while driving); (d) strategic/educated guessing has two named sub-techniques — elimination (rule out an option the audio clearly contradicts, raising odds from ~33% to ~50%) and context-based prediction (guess the semantically plausible word even if focus was lost, since a blank is guaranteed wrong); (e) use the test's own played example recording (where given) specifically to tune your ear to that speaker's accent before the real questions start, since accent — not vocabulary or grammar — is named as the main value of the example recording.

Given the 46-second length, no procedural detail is available — this is a title/concept only. (q7xCHfDRdug's transcript does not use this term, so it remains unresolved.)
