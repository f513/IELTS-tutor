## 2. Techniques & strategies taught

Distinct named/numbered techniques, now split between **confirmed-by-transcript** (the two new sources) and **still description-only** (everything else, unchanged from the original pass):

### Confirmed by transcript

**Three core reading skills (OtmUQwPVLko) — resolves the old "skimming/scanning" gap:**
- **Skimming** — reading a paragraph quickly for its *general meaning*. Used mainly for Matching Headings.
- **Scanning** — reading only to *find the location* of the answer (via keywords/synonyms), explicitly **not** to decide the answer itself: "we would scan for those words from the question or synonyms from those words not to find the correct answer but to find the location of the correct answer."
- **Close reading** — reading carefully, word-by-word, the specific section once its location is found, in order to actually decide the answer. The video calls this "the most important skill" and "the skill most overlooked by about six or about 7 or band 8 student[s]." The channel's answer to "skimming/scanning isn't enough" is literally: skim and scan only get you to the right spot; close reading is what gets you the right answer.
- A general principle used across almost every question type in both transcripts: **"where before what"** — find *where* the answer is in the text before deciding *what* the answer is.

**True/False/Not Given — step-by-step method (previously "mechanics unknown," now resolved):**
Both transcripts give essentially the same method; `OtmUQwPVLko`'s is the more detailed "VIP" version.
1. Read the instructions; confirm whether it's True/False/Not Given (fact-based) or Yes/No/Not Given (writer's-opinion-based) — the method is the same, but YNNG is judged against what the *writer believes*, not what is factually true. Watch for mixing up the writer's opinion with a different person's opinion mentioned in the same text.
2. Read **each full question statement**, not just keywords. `3KDP8P-pvEw`: "read the whole statements first... don't focus in on keywords... because if you are just focusing in on keywords, you won't understand the sentence, which means you will never be able to say if it's false or true or not given." `OtmUQwPVLko` adds: pay attention to **qualifying words** that modify nouns (e.g. "some" vs. "all," "female polar bears" vs. "male brown bears") — these alone can flip True into False.
3. Think of likely **synonyms** the passage might use for the statement's content words (e.g. "bone density" → "skeleton"; "buildup of fat" → "overweight").
4. **Scan** for the location using those words/synonyms — not to answer yet, just to find the spot.
5. Re-read the statement, then **close-read** that section of text carefully.
6. Decide: text agrees with the statement → **True**; text contradicts it → **False**; text says nothing that lets you judge either way → **Not Given**. `3KDP8P-pvEw`'s own summary: "if the meaning matches... it's true. If the text says [the opposite]... it's false. If the statement says [X] and the text says nothing about [X]... we've no idea... so put not given."
- Explicit anti-pattern called out in both videos: don't hunt for "not given" as if searching for a specific object that must be somewhere — "you are searching for something that is not there," which wastes time that then damages the rest of the section.
- `OtmUQwPVLko` adds a personalization warm-up exercise taught to VIP students: write a true fact about yourself, then write one *true*, one *false*, and one *not-given* statement about that fact, to internalize the distinction before applying it to real passages (demoed live with the instructor's own nationality/age). It also adds: ignore all outside/real-world knowledge — judge only what the text says, nothing you independently know to be true; if stuck, default-mark NG and move on, then revisit if time allows; and, as a soft cross-check only (not a hard rule), a block of TFNG questions will "always" include at least one of each answer type.

**Matching Headings — step-by-step method (previously "mechanics unknown," now resolved):**
Both transcripts converge on the same core technique; `OtmUQwPVLko` gives the fuller walkthrough (demoed live on an AI-themed passage).
1. If Matching Headings appears anywhere in a section, **do it first**, before the section's other questions — because it requires reading the whole passage anyway, which then makes every other question in that section easier. `3KDP8P-pvEw`: "Do this question first... because it's going to make the other questions easier."
2. **Do not look at the heading list first.** Read each paragraph on its own.
3. For each paragraph, **write your own short heading** in your own words before looking at the official options. Both videos stress this forces genuine understanding of the paragraph's *general* meaning rather than keyword-spotting: "this forces you to understand the meaning of the whole paragraph, which is what they're testing" (`3KDP8P-pvEw`).
4. Only then read the official heading list, paying attention to the differences in meaning between similar-sounding options. `OtmUQwPVLko` adds a physical trick for near-duplicate headings: cover the others with a pen/ruler, or rewrite the two or three similar ones side by side, to compare them directly.
5. **Match the obvious ones first** — any paragraph where your own heading is close or identical to an official heading is very likely correct immediately.
6. For the remaining paragraphs, use **elimination**: rule out headings that clearly don't fit, then close-read the paragraph again to decide between the 2–3 that remain.
7. If stuck, don't dwell — guess and move on (losing one question rarely costs a band, especially on Academic, where one wrong answer out of 40 can still be a 9).
- Explicit anti-pattern in both transcripts: reading only the first/last sentence of a paragraph, or hunting for keywords, is the opposite of what's being tested — the question tests whole-paragraph general meaning, not detail.

## 8. Agent brief — what a Reading specialist AI agent should know/do

- **Teach one strategy per question type, never a single universal trick.** Now transcript-confirmed as the channel's core philosophy, stated nearly verbatim by the instructor himself, not just inferred from marketing copy.
- **Teach the three-skill model explicitly: skim → scan → close-read.** Skim for a paragraph's general meaning (mainly for Matching Headings); scan only to locate the answer's position using synonyms, never to decide the answer; close-read the located section carefully to actually decide the answer. Flag close reading as the skill most students skip and the one that actually produces correct answers.
- **Teach the channel's real Matching Headings method, attributed to this channel with confidence (no longer generic):** if Matching Headings appears in a section, do it first. Read each paragraph without looking at the heading list; write your own short heading per paragraph; only then read the official headings and compare their differences in meaning; match any paragraph where your own heading is close/identical to an official one immediately; for the rest, eliminate clearly-wrong headings and close-read to decide between what's left.
- **Teach the channel's real True/False/Not Given (and Yes/No/Not Given) method, attributed to this channel with confidence (no longer generic):** read each full question statement (not just keywords) and note any qualifying words ("some" vs. "all," etc.); predict likely synonyms; scan for location only; close-read that section; decide True (agrees), False (contradicts), or Not Given (text doesn't address it) based purely on the text, never on outside/real-world knowledge. For Yes/No/Not Given, apply the identical process but judge against the writer's stated opinion, not fact. If genuinely unsure, mark NG provisionally and move on rather than searching indefinitely for something that may not be there.
- **Cover all 12 question types** (Sentence Completion, Summary Completion, Multiple Choice, Short Answer, Labelling a Diagram, True/False/Not Given, Yes/No/Not Given, Matching Sentences, Matching Names, Matching Information, Table/Flowchart Completion, Matching Headings) — now transcript-confirmed as the channel's deliberately taught catalog, not just description-inferred. Still mention to the student/dev that one untranscribed source video says "11" — treat that as an unexplained outlier in a video we still can't verify, not a reason to doubt the 12-type list.
- **Flag Short Answer and Matching Sentences as comparatively rare question types** on the real test, per the instructor's own framing, so the agent can calibrate how much practice time to allocate relative to the more common types.
- **Frame the Reading test as substantially a vocabulary/synonym test, with a real method attached:** recommend active reading (reading with the specific intent to notice and log unfamiliar words or fix a known personal weakness) and keeping a personal vocabulary notebook — unknown word → guess its meaning from context first → look up the real meaning and synonyms → log it. This is no longer just a stated belief; it is the channel's actual prescribed method.
- **Push the channel's real 5-step diagnose-then-fix loop, not a generic version of it:** (1) get 2–3 real/official practice tests only (Cambridge English, British Council, or IDP — explicitly warn against third-party "free practice test" sites as unreliable); (2) take them under genuine timed exam conditions, no peeking; (3) mark honestly against the real answer key, including spelling/silly mistakes; (4) find the *pattern* behind the wrong answers (spelling? vocabulary? a specific question type/strategy? running out of time?) rather than treating a low score as generic "being bad at reading"; (5) actively drill that one identified weakness before moving on, rather than re-doing full tests blindly.
- **Teach the "practice slow, then fast" progression** when introducing a new strategy or fixing a weakness: have the student apply the new technique at a deliberately reduced pace first (even minutes per question) to internalize it correctly, then gradually increase speed across sessions until the strategy becomes automatic — explicitly compared by the instructor to riding a bike or tying shoelaces.
- **Teach real time-management guidance, not generic pacing advice:** the three passages get progressively harder by design (Section 1 easiest, Section 3 hardest, meant to separate Band 7/8/9 at the margin), so recommend spending *less* time on Section 1 and allocating more time to Sections 2–3, rather than splitting time evenly across all three.
- **Tell students that strong general English does not guarantee a good Reading score** — now with a transcript-confirmed specific cause to point to (poor spelling, and more fundamentally vocabulary/synonym gaps), not just the general claim.
- **Treat General Training and Academic Reading as the same question types and strategies but different text register**, and be precise about the scoring nuance: General Training needs 40/40 for a Band 9; Academic allows one wrong (39/40) for a Band 9.
- **Do not fabricate step counts, named techniques, or rule details that still aren't in this dataset.** The "5 step strategy," "4 steps," and "5 reading skills" named systems remain unconfirmed — if referenced, say explicitly that the specifics are unknown rather than inventing plausible-sounding steps.
